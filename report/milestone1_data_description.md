## Data

We built our dataset from 300 arXiv papers: 100 each from Computation and Language (cs.CL), Machine Learning (cs.LG) and Computer Vision (cs.CV), downloaded through the arXiv API. To avoid over-representing a single period or trend, we sampled papers across all 48 months from 2022 to 2025 and kept only papers whose primary category matched, so no paper appears in two domains. We extracted body paragraphs from each PDF in reading order with PyMuPDF. During extraction we dropped references, captions, running headers, and table and equation fragments, and we recorded the section each paragraph came from. Three papers had too little clean text and were removed, leaving 297.

Each example simulates a writer partway through a paragraph. We sampled up to five target paragraphs per paper and cut each one at a random word. The text before the cut is the *prefix*, and the author's next 10, 30 or 60 words are the *target*. The *context* is every paragraph that comes before the target paragraph in the paper, which is the text the writer has already produced. The target paragraph and everything after it are never included, so the model cannot copy the answer. This setup follows the standard self-supervised recipe for completion data used in language modeling and fill-in-the-middle training: real text is truncated and the original continuation serves as the reference.

We split the data 80/10/10 by paper within each domain, so no paper or its context appears in more than one split. This gives 1,182 training examples from 237 papers, 150 development examples from 30 papers, and 150 test examples from 30 papers. Examples are balanced across domains and target lengths (554 of 10 words, 548 of 30 words, 380 of 60 words), and each has about 30 context paragraphs on average. The data is stored as JSON Lines, with one example per line containing the prefix, target, target length, section, domain and context paragraphs. The main limitations come from PDF extraction: some paragraphs keep inline math symbols, and section names are not standardized across papers.
